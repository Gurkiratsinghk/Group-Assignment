# Step 4: Type I/II Errors and Power Analysis

## Part A: Business interpretation

**Type I error in this context:** we conclude the IT basket's average daily range exceeds 2.0% when it truly does not. The desk reacts to a false alarm — stops are widened, margin buffers are raised, capital gets tied up in unused buffer, and position sizes shrink to stay within the new (unnecessarily conservative) risk limits. The cost is a real, ongoing drag on returns for a risk that was never actually there.

**Type II error in this context:** we fail to detect that the true average range exceeds 2.0% when it genuinely does. Stops stay set at the old, too-tight level and get triggered by ordinary day-to-day noise rather than real directional moves; risk limits understate real exposure; and on a genuinely volatile day, losses exceed the modelled worst case because the desk's own risk model was quietly wrong.

**Which error should this desk fear more?** The Type II error is the more dangerous one here. A false alarm (Type I) costs some capital efficiency — annoying, but bounded and recoverable. Missing a genuine increase in volatility (Type II) risks under-collateralized positions and losses that exceed what the risk model assumed possible, which is a tail-risk, capital-adequacy problem, not just a performance drag. This is a judgement about the relative cost of the two mistakes, not a statistical fact, and it argues for a desk that leans toward *higher* alpha (more willing to tolerate false alarms) or, more precisely, toward monitoring power and beta explicitly rather than fixating on alpha alone — statistics can quantify the trade-off, but only business judgement about consequences can decide where to sit on it.

## Part B: What the power analysis actually shows

The observed effect size in our n = 40 sample is tiny: Cohen's d = (2.0041 − 2.0) / 1.1597 ≈ 0.0036, because the sample mean landed almost exactly on the 2.0% benchmark. At that effect size, our n = 40 test has power of only 0.052 — barely above alpha itself — meaning beta ≈ 0.948. In plain terms: if the true mean is only fractionally above 2.0%, this test was never going to catch it. Reaching 80% power at this effect size would require roughly 489,000 observations, and 90% power roughly 677,000 — both wildly impractical, which is itself the finding: our test result should be read as "no detectable signal," not "the risk is confirmed at 2.0%." The 10,000-trial Monte Carlo check on the n=40, d=0.5 grid cell (power 0.9335 vs. the analytic 0.9281) confirms the analytic power values are trustworthy where the effect size is meaningfully larger than zero.

**Sample size and beta move in opposite directions.** Larger n shrinks the standard error (s/√n), so the same absolute gap between the sample mean and 2.0% produces a larger t-statistic and more power (lower beta) — that is exactly why the grid shows power rising sharply with n at d = 0.5 and 0.8. But more data here means reaching further back in trading history, which risks blending in a different volatility regime (a pre-2024 period, a different rate environment, a different set of index constituents) — the extra n buys statistical power at the cost of a less homogeneous sample. And at very large n, even a tiny, economically meaningless deviation from 2.0% becomes statistically significant; the desk should not confuse a rejected null at n in the hundreds of thousands with a finding that actually matters for how stops should be set.
