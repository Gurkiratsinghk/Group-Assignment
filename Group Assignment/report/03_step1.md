# Step 1: Point Estimates from a Single Sample (n = 40)

A simple random sample of 40 sessions drawn without replacement from the population (seed 42) gives a sample mean range_pct of 2.00% against a true population mean of 2.07% — a sampling error of only -0.066 percentage points, small purely by luck of the draw, not because 40 observations guarantee precision. The sample standard deviation is 1.16%, and dividing by √40 gives a standard error of the mean of 0.183%.

In plain business terms: if we repeated this exact sampling procedure many times, the sample mean would typically land within about ±0.18 percentage points of the true average daily trading range, and about 95% of such samples would produce a mean within roughly ±0.37 points (≈2 SEs) of the truth. That is a reasonably tight band relative to a mean of ~2%, but it is not negligible — a single 40-day sample could plausibly report a "typical" daily range anywhere from about 1.6% to 2.4% just from sampling noise, even if the underlying market behavior hasn't changed at all. For a risk desk deciding whether today's volatility regime looks normal, that means a one-off 40-day estimate is directionally useful but should not be treated as precise to the tenth of a percentage point; a materially different reading (say, 2.5% or higher) is more likely a real signal, while a small shift within the ±0.2 range could easily be noise.

This also underscores why the ddof=1 correction matters: using ddof=0 here would have understated s (1.145 vs 1.160) and, propagated through, understated the standard error itself — making the single sample look more trustworthy than it actually is, which is precisely the kind of quiet error that leads to overconfident business decisions.
